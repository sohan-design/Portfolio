Putting common sense back into iOS

TypeEssay
PublishedJun 2026
TopicsiOS · UX · Product

It’s not about what phone, OS, or device you use every day. It’s 2026, and with or without AI, technology should make life easier and smoother in every moment.

I used a Google Pixel for years. Regardless of what hardware you can buy at any price point, Pixel’s hardware was never its USP. The software was.

It was so good and so effortless to use that it simply got out of your way. You didn’t think about it. You just flowed with it. It was like breathing, completely involuntary.

And the best part?

Most of that experience had nothing to do with AI.

Some of the things I’ll talk about in this article come from different operating systems, Android skins, brands, and apps that I’ve used over the years. They’re small experiences that stayed with me because they solved real problems with simple thinking.

This year, I gifted myself an iPhone.

And honestly, I’ve never been less happy with a phone purchase.

I’m a UX designer by profession, and seeing such beautiful interfaces paired with such frustrating user experiences finally pushed me to write this article.

***********

Common sense things

Let’s start chronologically with my day.

***********

Waking up

I wake up around 8 AM.

For context, I set multiple alarms because I like to snooze. But I also want to wake up eventually. Usually, I wake up by the second or third alarm. Sometimes I even wake up before the alarm goes off.

Now, for any sane smartphone, it should understand one simple thing. If I’m actively using my phone, I’m awake.

This doesn’t require state-of-the-art AI. It doesn’t require an LLM. It’s common sense. An A18 chip, or even a child’s brain, could figure this out.

A simple “Dismiss All Remaining Alarms” CTA after the first alarm would solve the problem instantly. Instead, the phone happily continues ringing every backup alarm I’ve ever set, even though I’m already brushing my teeth.

***********

Notification Center

Now I unlock my phone. There are 50 notifications waiting for me.

I’ve been using an iPhone for six months, and I can honestly say I’ve opened Notification Center fewer than ten times. It’s that bad.

Notifications feel endless, poorly grouped, and difficult to process. Ironically, I do notice notifications in real time when they arrive. But the moment I’m inactive for a while, they pile up into a giant wall of information that I simply ignore.

I’ve never ignored a notification center before.

A combination of better grouping by app, smarter categorization, and modern prioritization could solve most of this. And somehow, in 2026, iOS still doesn’t support notification channels.

It’s surprising because notifications are one of the most important interactions on a smartphone.

***********

The flashlight incident

Then I ride my bike to the office. About thirty minutes into my commute, I feel warmth in my pocket. My phone is getting hot. Really hot. I pull it out and realize the flashlight is on. How? No idea.

At some point, it got activated accidentally and stayed on long enough to turn my pocket into a portable oven. I had to stop in traffic, unlock my phone, and manually turn it off.

Physical discomfort caused by a flashlight shouldn’t be a thing. A simple auto-turn-off after five minutes would save battery, prevent overheating, and probably save a lot of thighs.

And before anyone says this is a rare issue, I regularly see people on public transport with their flashlight accidentally turned on.

***********

Let me select text

Later, I’m scrolling through social media. I find a useful link buried in the comments. My phone has clearly rendered the text. It can display it. It knows it’s text. Yet I can’t select it.

I miss desktops.

Global text selection feels like such a simple feature, but only a few companies do it well. Being able to select text from anywhere — images, recent apps, screenshots, social feeds — sounds small until you’ve used it. Then it becomes difficult to live without.

***********

Navigation shouldn’t be a puzzle

Have you ever used iOS and thought: “Wow, this looks beautiful.” Followed immediately by: “How do I go back?”

Sometimes the back button sits all the way in the top-left corner, miles away from your thumb. Then there are gesture-based navigation patterns that work only some of the time. And occasionally, the only reliable way out is to go back to the home screen and start over.

How is this still a thing?

You’re a trillion-dollar company. Surely we can agree that users shouldn’t have to guess how to navigate between screens. A platform should have a universal navigation language.

***********

The keyboard

I’ve installed Gboard on my iPhone. That alone should tell you everything.

I don’t want to spend too much time criticizing Apple’s keyboard, but it’s genuinely frustrating to use. The autocorrect is aggressive, inconsistent, and often feels like it’s working against me.

More importantly, it doesn’t seem to learn. I’ve typed some of my friends’ names hundreds of times. Why am I still correcting the same mistakes?

We’re carrying incredibly powerful mobile processors in our pockets. Remembering a frequently used name shouldn’t be a challenge.

And while we’re here: Where is the number row?

***********

Context matters

Remember those alarms I mentioned earlier? I often forget whether they’re still enabled. When is my next alarm? Did I turn it off? Did I accidentally disable tomorrow’s wake-up alarm? So I open the Clock app just to check. Again. And again. And again.

The thing is, I always look at my lock screen before sleeping. That’s where context should live. Imagine if iOS simply showed:

“Next alarm: 7:00 AM.” Done.

No taps. No app launches. No mental effort. The same idea extends far beyond alarms.

Let’s say I have a flight in 18 hours.

A small contextual widget could remind me about it, show airport information, estimate when I should leave, and surface useful details exactly when I need them.

This idea is heavily inspired by Google’s At a Glance feature. It’s one of those features that’s so good that once you’ve experienced it, you want it everywhere.

***********

And then there’s everything else

There are plenty of other things to complain about.

  • The lack of a built-in clipboard manager
  • The fact that WhatsApp on iOS feels like a collection of UX compromises
  • A closed ecosystem that turns storage into a forced iCloud upsell

Take WhatsApp backups, for example. On iOS, you’re effectively tied to iCloud. If you run out of storage, your solution is simple: buy more iCloud storage. That’s it.

In a world full of cloud providers and storage options, users are still pushed toward a single ecosystem. It feels less like a user-first decision and more like a business one.

***********

Intelligence doesn’t need AI

The irony is that most of these problems don’t require artificial intelligence to solve.

They require common sense.

The smartest products I’ve used weren’t necessarily the most advanced. They were the ones that understood context, reduced friction, and respected my time. That’s the kind of intelligence I want from my phone.

Not another chatbot. Just pure common sense.
