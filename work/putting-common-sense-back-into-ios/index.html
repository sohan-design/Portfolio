Good design, bad UX What an OS taught me about UX

TypeEssay
PublishedJun 16, 2026
TopicsiOS · UX · Product

Six months ago, I bought an iPhone 17.

A month in, I wrote a long rant about everything I hated about it. Six months later, I still agree with a lot of it. But I also realise I was looking at the wrong thing.

The iPhone isn’t a bad phone. In fact, some parts of it are ridiculously good. The hardware is excellent. Performance is absurd. Battery life is great. Haptics are unmatched. Face ID is brilliant. Video is fantastic. iOS animations are probably the best in the business. Apps are polished. And the thing is reliably boring — it just works.

Yet somehow, I still find myself fighting the OS.

And as a product designer, that’s what interests me.

Good design doesn’t always mean good UX.
***********

A quick disclaimer

This isn’t an objective iPhone review. Most of my opinions come from comparing the iPhone 17 with my old Google Pixel 6a, which cost roughly one-third of the iPhone 17.

That’s obviously an unfair hardware comparison. I’m not comparing processors, cameras or benchmark scores. I’m comparing something much simpler:

How much friction does this phone add to my everyday life?

***********

I Miss the Invisible Phone

The Pixel 6a wasn’t impressive because of its hardware. It was impressive because the software mostly stayed out of my way.

It felt like using a phone rather than operating one.

That’s what I’ve started calling the Invisible Phone.

You shouldn’t have to think about the phone. You should think about what you’re trying to do.

***********

Notifications: The Graveyard

This is still my biggest complaint.

Live notifications are great. The problem starts when they’re no longer live.

My Notification Centre becomes a giant pile of things I don’t care about anymore. I’ve reached a point where I barely open it.

That’s probably the biggest UX failure: the interface has trained me not to use it.

iOS has notification grouping, scheduled summaries and AI-powered notification summaries on supported devices. But I still want something more fundamental: better information architecture.

I don’t want 40 notifications. I want three things that matter.

***********

Alarms: Just Tell Me What’s Next

The alarm experience is fine. But I still want one stupidly simple thing:

Next alarm · 4:20 AM

Put it on the Lock Screen. Put it in a widget. Put it somewhere.

I shouldn’t have to open the Clock app just to remember whether I turned tomorrow’s alarm on.

Small pieces of context like this are what made Google’s At a Glance so good.

***********

The Flashlight Shouldn’t Cook Me

This hasn’t changed.

I’ve accidentally turned on the flashlight. It stayed on. My phone got ridiculously hot in my pocket.

Why can’t the OS simply turn it off after five or ten minutes of inactivity?

It’s a tiny problem. But UX is made of tiny problems.

Good UX makes accidental states harmless.

***********

Back Navigation: Still Scary

This is probably my biggest iOS interaction complaint.

Sometimes there’s a back button. Sometimes there’s a gesture. Sometimes there’s a close button. Sometimes the gesture doesn’t work.

And sometimes I’m deep inside an interface thinking:

“How the hell do I get out of here?”

A navigation system shouldn’t make me understand the app’s architecture before I can go back.

***********

The Keyboard Is Beautiful. I Still Hate It.

Apple’s keyboard looks great. I don’t enjoy typing on it.

The missing number row is annoying. Autocorrect can be aggressively wrong. And after typing someone’s name 100 times, I’d expect the phone to understand that I probably meant to type that name.

So I installed Gboard.

On an iPhone.

That probably says enough.

***********

Text Selection Should Be Universal

If my phone can see text, I should be able to interact with it.

Sometimes I see a URL or a piece of text inside an app and can’t simply select it. This is one area where I still miss desktop-style interaction.

Text is text. Let me grab it.

***********

Widgets: Useful, But Why So Huge?

iOS widgets are useful. The ecosystem of third-party widgets is even better.

But why are so many of them enormous?

Sometimes I want information, not a billboard.

Give me density. Give me options.

***********

WhatsApp + iOS = Pain

The actual messaging experience is fine.

The migration and import experience? Terrible.

Moving between ecosystems shouldn’t feel like moving houses.

My data should feel like my data, not something attached to whichever platform I currently use.

***********

And Then There’s iCloud

I understand the ecosystem. I even like parts of it.

But there’s an uncomfortable trade-off:

The better the ecosystem becomes, the harder it becomes to leave.

Photos, backups, WhatsApp, documents, storage — everything slowly becomes part of the Apple ecosystem.

That’s great when you’re staying. Not so great when you’re trying to leave.

***********

The Things Apple Absolutely Nails

This is where my original rant was unfair.

Performance — Goat. I don’t think about performance. That’s the point. Everything is fast.

Battery — Goat. I trust the battery. That’s more valuable than a benchmark number.

Haptics — Goat. Apple’s haptics are ridiculously good. You feel them everywhere without thinking about them.

Face ID — Goat. One of those features you don’t appreciate until you don’t have it.

Build Quality — Goat. The hardware feels premium in a way that’s difficult to explain until you’ve used it for months.

Video — Excellent. I still prefer Google’s approach to photos, but iPhone video is something I trust. Point. Shoot. Good result.

iOS Animations — Still the Goat. This is where Apple is almost annoyingly good. The transitions, gestures and micro-interactions feel incredibly polished.

***********

Apple Intelligence: Meh

It’s 2026. And somehow, AI still hasn’t fundamentally changed how I use my phone.

Apple Intelligence is interesting, but for me, it isn’t transformative.

I don’t need my phone to become an AI chatbot. I want it to quietly understand context and remove friction.

That’s the AI I actually want.

***********

What Six Months Changed

My original argument was:

“Why am I paying three times more for this?”

I don’t think that anymore.

The iPhone is clearly a better piece of hardware than the Pixel 6a.

But that’s not what makes this comparison interesting.

The Pixel taught me that software can make mediocre hardware feel great.

The iPhone taught me that great hardware can make an operating system incredibly reliable.

Both are valuable.

***********

The Real Lesson

After six months, I don’t think iOS is bad.

I think it’s beautifully designed in ways that don’t always translate into the least-frictional experience.

And that’s an important distinction.

As designers, we sometimes optimise for:

Consistency → Beauty → System rules

when users care about:

Intent → Action → Outcome

The best interface isn’t necessarily the prettiest one.

It’s the one that makes you forget there was an interface at all.

The best phone isn’t the one you admire. It’s the one you don’t have to think about.

That’s what my Pixel taught me.

And that’s probably the thing I miss most.
