Design Engineering: The New AI Process

TypeEssay
PublishedSep 29, 2026
TopicsAI · Design · Process

AI has completely changed the way I approach product design.

Earlier, my workflow was pretty straightforward: understand the problem, research, design in Figma, and hand it over to developers.

Now, I use AI to explore ideas, build rough prototypes, test functionality, and figure out what works before I start designing the actual UI.

It’s not about giving AI a prompt and getting a perfect product. There’s a lot of back and forth, things that don’t work, and decisions that still need a designer’s eye.

Here’s how my workflow looks today.

***********

Step 1: Brain Dump

Whenever I start working on a new feature, I don’t immediately jump into Figma.

I get everything out of my head first. Meeting notes, a conversation with a coworker, a conversation with myself, or just holding the dictation keys while I think through the problem with whatever I already know.

My favorite dictation tool is @WillowVoiceAI. I’m using it right now to write this.

I dump all of that into ChatGPT or Claude, along with product documentation, user feedback, requirements, and anything else that might matter.

I’m not trying to organize it yet. I just want as much context as possible.

***********

Step 2: Scope and Plan

Once I have all the information in one place, I start working with AI to break down the problem.

I’ll ask questions, explore different scenarios, identify gaps, and figure out what we’re actually trying to build.

Sometimes, explaining the problem to AI helps me realize that I haven’t thought through certain things properly.

I’ll also use it for research, competitor analysis, organizing requirements, and creating documents or tables.

The goal is to understand the problem and define the scope before building anything.

***********

Step 3: Build the First Version

This is where I use tools like Cursor, Lovable, v0, or Google Stitch to build a rough, functional prototype.

And honestly, I already know the first version is going to look bad.

The layout might be off, the hierarchy might not make sense, and there might be unnecessary components everywhere.

But that’s fine.

I just want something that works well enough to help me understand how the feature might function.

Instead of starting from a blank canvas in Figma, I now have something I can actually interact with.

***********

Step 4: Critique and Simplify

Once I have a working prototype, I start tearing it apart.

I look for unnecessary components, confusing layouts, missing screens, edge cases, and error states.

AI tends to add too much stuff—cards, icons, labels, colors, and sections that don’t always add value.

So I start removing things, simplifying the experience, and rethinking the information hierarchy.

The best part is that I’m not afraid to change everything.

I can ask AI to rebuild a section or try a completely different approach without worrying about reworking a bunch of screens manually.

The prototype is just a starting point, not something I need to protect.

***********

Step 5: Refine the Prototype

Once the basic experience makes sense, I use Cursor to improve the structure, functionality, and usability.

I’ll work on navigation, interactions, information architecture, and missing functionality until the prototype starts feeling like a real product.

But a working product doesn’t automatically mean it’s well-designed.

The UI can still feel inconsistent, components might not match, and adding new features can introduce more design inconsistencies.

You can keep refining the prototype forever, but at some point, you need to move on.

This stage is great for early stakeholder feedback and understanding how people might interact with the product.

***********

Step 6: Figma and Craft

Now I take the prototype, screenshots, and all the context I’ve gathered into Figma.

This is where I actually design the product.

I work on typography, spacing, visual hierarchy, design system components, tokens, and all the little details that make the experience feel consistent.

Honestly, going back to Figma can feel slow after building a functional prototype.

But this is where the actual craft happens.

I can explore different layouts, experiment with interactions, and come up with solutions I hadn’t considered earlier.

I also share designs and prototypes with the team to get feedback.

By this stage, a lot of the exploration is already done, so I often don’t need as many screens as I used to.

***********

Step 7: Build It Again

Once the designs are ready, I start a fresh conversation in Cursor and share the final Figma designs, requirements, and documentation.

Before building anything, I want to make sure AI understands what has changed and whether the new designs introduce any gaps or conflicts.

One of the biggest challenges here is translating Figma designs into working code.

A screenshot shows how a screen looks, but it doesn’t explain every interaction, component state, or edge case.

Ideally, I’d want AI to understand the component structure, design tokens, interactions, and how everything connects.

I also want it to ask questions and identify missing states, just like a developer would.

Once the prototype is functional and the core experience works, it’s time for handoff.

***********

Step 8: Handoff to Developers

I still believe working with a good developer is incredibly important, especially when building within an existing product.

Performance, APIs, code structure, integrations, and production dependencies require a level of technical context that goes beyond making the UI work.

I use AI for prototyping and smaller UI changes, but I still want developers involved in production implementation.

The handoff process hasn’t changed much. I share the final designs, explain the requirements, and work with developers to get the feature shipped.

The difference is that now they also get a working prototype they can interact with, instead of relying only on static screens.

***********

Final Thoughts

I don’t think AI has changed what product design actually is.

I’m still trying to understand problems, explore solutions, and create experiences that work for people.

What’s changed is how I get there.

I can now use AI to explore ideas, build prototypes, and test functionality before spending time crafting the final UI.

By the time I reach Figma, I already have a much clearer idea of what I’m trying to build.

The tools will keep changing, and this workflow might evolve, but the core process remains the same.

It’s still about exploring ideas, making decisions, and figuring out what works.

AI just gives me a different way to do it.
